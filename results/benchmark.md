## F. Largest instances: the SAT-sweeping crossover

Widths beyond 9 were measured individually rather than in the sweep above,
because a single row here takes hours. Timings come from separate long runs on
an otherwise-working machine, so treat them as indicative to roughly a factor
of two - the width-12 gap is far larger than that.

| width | AIG nodes | inputs | plain SAT (s) | SAT+sweep (s) | sweep merges | speed-up |
|-------|-----------|--------|---------------|---------------|--------------|----------|
| 8     | 1092      | 16     | 8.5           | 9.0           | 15           | 0.94x    |
| 9     | 1419      | 18     | 33.0          | 46.6          | 18           | 0.71x    |
| 10    | 1781      | 20     | not measured  | 96.8          | 18           | -        |
| 12    | 2652      | 24     | 11509.4       | 3227.3        | 21           | **3.57x** |

This is the most interesting result in the study, and it reverses the
conclusion drawn from the small widths alone.

Sweeping carries a fixed overhead - simulate, classify, then run dozens of
small equivalence solves - and at width 8 or 9 that overhead exceeds what the
merges save, so sweeping *loses*. But the plain miter is a single monolithic
UNSAT proof whose cost explodes with width (33 s at width 9, 11,509 s at width
12 - a factor of 350 for three extra bits), whereas sweeping decomposes the
same obligation into ~73 small solves whose total grows far more slowly. The
two curves cross somewhere between width 9 and width 12, and past the crossing
sweeping wins by a widening margin.

That is precisely the argument for SAT sweeping in industrial tools: it is not
that it helps on easy instances - it does not - but that it changes the shape
of the curve on hard ones. Note that the win arrives with only 21 merges out
of a 2,645-node cone, so it is not bulk simplification doing the work; it is
that a few well-placed internal equivalences break one intractable proof into
many tractable ones.

## G. Algebraic backend: polynomial reduction instead of search

Multipliers are where CDCL collapses, so this backend abandons search entirely. Every gate becomes a polynomial, the arithmetic specification becomes a polynomial, and verification is reduction: substitute each gate variable by its definition in reverse topological order and check that everything cancels to zero. Following the AMulet line of work (Kaufmann, Biere, Kauers).

Note this proves each design against the *specification* rather than against the other design, so no miter is built at all.

| width | gates | peak polynomial terms | algebraic (s) | plain SAT (s) |
|-------|-------|-----------------------|---------------|---------------|
| 8     | 612   | 221                   | 0.015         | 8.5           |
| 12    | 1492  | 309                   | 0.034         | 11509.4       |
| 16    | 2756  | 429                   | 0.069         | not reachable |
| 24    | 6436  | 765                   | 0.225         | not reachable |
| 32    | 11652 | 1229                  | 0.578         | not reachable |
| 48    | 26692 | 2541                  | 2.467         | not reachable |
| 64    | 47876 | 4365                  | 7.639         | not reachable |

The 12x12 case is the headline: **11,509 s by SAT, 0.034 s algebraically** - a factor of roughly 340,000. A 64x64 multiplier with 47,876 gates and 128 primary inputs is proved in 7.6 s, a problem no amount of CDCL would ever finish.

Peak polynomial size grows about quadratically in the width (221 terms at 8 bits, 4,365 at 64), which is why the method scales where search does not.

### The catch: proving is cheap, refuting is not

The method is asymmetric, and badly so. On a *correct* 6x6 multiplier the intermediate polynomial peaks at 186 terms and finishes in 0.01 s. On a *broken* one - a single partial product shifted by the wrong amount - nothing cancels, and the same reduction peaks at 621,004 terms and takes 33 s.

That is the opposite profile to random simulation, which refutes bugs in microseconds and can never prove anything. The two are complementary rather than competing: simulate to find bugs, reduce polynomials to prove correctness.
